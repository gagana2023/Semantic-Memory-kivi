# Inverting EMMA: the decisions behind *Mixed-Session Conversation with Egocentric Memory*

**Paper:** Jang, Kim & Kim, *Mixed-Session Conversation with Egocentric Memory* (arXiv:2410.02503, 2024), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02503)  
**Compared against:** *The Things Kivi Comes to Know* (attached position document).  
**Method note:** This is a decision map, not a set of recommendations. “Holds” means the paper’s assumption matches Kivi’s stated position; “does not hold” identifies the direction of the mismatch. “Underspecified” means the paper does not provide enough information to infer an answer safely.

## 1. Decision inventory

| ID | Decision question | Paper’s answer | Defensible alternatives rejected or not considered | Assumptions in the paper’s setting | Fit for Kivi | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | **When conversation spans time and people, what unit should organize the interaction history?** | Six-session episodes centered on one main speaker, with one of three partners appearing in each session. | One persistent dyad; true multi-party sessions; topic-, project-, or task-centered history; event streams without session boundaries. | Exactly four synthetic personas; six sessions; one partner at a time; continuity is the primary goal. | **Does not hold.** Kivi receives dictation, application context, and Hey Kivi requests; work entities/projects and source events can cut across sessions, and multiple third parties can appear in one input. The difference pushes away from partner/session as the only organizing key. | §§1, 3, especially §3.1 and Fig. 2, pp. 1–3; limitation, p. 9. |
| D2 | **Whose point of view should determine what a shared interaction becomes in memory?** | The main speaker’s egocentric perspective; memories are written separately “about me” and about each partner. | Neutral event record; source-speaker perspective; per-claim attribution; multiple simultaneous perspectives; user-only memory. | A conversational agent embodies the main speaker and may preserve its view of every partner. | **Partly holds.** Kivi is user-centered, but requires every belief to be source-attributable and forbids durable personal memory about others. Egocentric ownership fits; partner profiling does not. | §3.3, pp. 3–4; §4 and Fig. 3, p. 4. Kivi §§1, 5, 9. |
| D3 | **Which parts of a completed interaction are important enough to write into long-term memory?** | At each session end, a model extracts significant events, experiences, appointments, and expressed emotions; it may output no memory. | User-declared saves only; work facts/preferences/entities only; novelty or future-utility tests; consent-sensitive classes; verbatim episodic archive; extraction triggered by later need. | Emotional and personal details improve future open-domain conversation; storage harm is low; importance can be inferred from one session. | **Does not hold.** Kivi hard-excludes sensitive subjects, moods/emotions, relationships/family, and characterizations, and asks whether a durable work fact saves repetition. The mismatch pushes toward a narrower candidate set and explicit retention policy. | §3.3 “Memory Generation,” pp. 3–4; §4.1 “Memory Summarizer,” p. 4; Appendix C/Table 18. Kivi §§2–5. |
| D4 | **When an input contains information about another person, what may survive after the immediate task?** | Partner-specific experiences, thoughts, emotions, health, relationships, and other personal information may be summarized and retained from the main speaker’s perspective. | Do not retain third-party content; retain only consented facts; retain only work-level residue; redact sensitive categories; isolate it with access/expiry controls. | Fictional/synthetic partners have no consent or privacy interests; richer recall is beneficial. Failure cost is conversational inconsistency, not privacy harm. | **Does not hold—directly opposed.** Kivi’s write path must structurally reject third-party personal content while still allowing it in transient context. The difference pushes retention sharply downward. | §3.3, p. 3; live-chat example/Table 5, pp. 6–7; examples Tables 13–15, pp. 17–19; Ethics, p. 9. Kivi §§2 and 5. |
| D5 | **When should memory extraction run, and what context should it see?** | Once at the end of every session, using the entire session history, separately for self and partner. | Per turn; streaming candidate log; delayed batch; hybrid extraction; extraction only on explicit save or task boundary; cross-session consolidation. | Session endings are known; sessions fit within a 1,024-token dialogue-module input; end-of-session latency is acceptable. | **Does not hold cleanly.** Kivi explicitly leaves per-transcript vs batch vs hybrid open, and its inputs may be long or lack natural session boundaries. The paper pushes toward convenient batch boundaries but supplies no evidence for Kivi’s workload. | §4.1 “Memory Summarizer,” p. 4; Appendix E.1, p. 12. Kivi Appendix B. |
| D6 | **How should memories be represented so later conversation can use them?** | Short natural-language memory sentences, separated by `[SEP]`, partitioned by speaker, with binary links among related sentences. | Typed records; atomic propositions; event/entity graphs; source spans plus metadata; embeddings over raw transcript chunks; tiered epistemic objects. | A 32-token encoder input per memory is sufficient; free text can carry identity, temporality, confidence, and change implicitly. | **Does not hold.** Kivi commits to explicit `type`, epistemic `tier`, evidence count, source IDs, timestamps, and status. The difference pushes from prose-only memories toward typed, inspectable objects. | §§3.3–4.2, pp. 3–5; Appendix E.2, p. 12. Kivi §4. |
| D7 | **When several statements concern the same evolving situation, should the system replace history, preserve it, or connect it?** | Preserve every memory and link related/updated ones; use the newest retrieved item as a guide to associated past memories rather than deleting or rewriting them. | Destructive update; versioned supersession; temporal validity intervals; contradiction sets; confidence-weighted reconciliation; ask the user. | Information loss is worse than retaining stale or conflicting material; links make evolution intelligible to the generator. | **Partly conflicts.** Kivi also preserves audit history, but says contradictions supersede rather than stack and user demotion must block re-derivation. Paper-style links alone push toward resurfacing stale content. | §4.1 “Memory Linker,” pp. 4–5; §3.3 “Memory Connection,” p. 3. Kivi §9. |
| D8 | **What should count as a relationship between two memories?** | A binary “positive” link if they share context or one updates a specific event; otherwise “negative”; links may cross speakers. | Typed edges (supports, contradicts, supersedes, caused-by, same-entity); graded similarity; temporal/entity rules; no graph; human-confirmed links. | Relatedness is sufficiently captured by a binary classifier, and different semantic relations can be treated alike during retrieval. | **Does not hold for Kivi’s stated mechanics.** Kivi distinguishes confirmation, correction, supersession, demotion, and suppression; collapsing these into “related” removes action semantics. | §3.3 “Memory Connection,” p. 3; §4.1 “Memory Linker,” pp. 4–5; memory-pair examples, Table 15. |
| D9 | **When evidence conflicts, what should determine which account governs the next response?** | The latest/retrieved memory guides access to linked history; beyond linking, conflict resolution policy is **underspecified**. | Recency; source authority; user confirmation; confidence; explicit contradiction detection; abstention; show competing accounts. | Synthetic scenarios are designed for coherent updates; contradictions are relatively clean and conversational. | **Does not hold.** Kivi needs user-correctable beliefs with tier, provenance, supersession, and sticky suppression. Real work sources can conflict, so the unspecified policy becomes load-bearing. | §4.1, especially pp. 4–5; consistency claims in §6, p. 6. Kivi §§4, 8, 9. |
| D10 | **When memory grows, what should be discarded or lose standing?** | Nothing is deleted or directly updated for relevance; memories are retained and connected. Dataset construction discards malformed/toxic episodes, not aging memories. | TTL/decay; salience pruning; compression; archival tiers; user deletion; capacity budgets; purpose-based expiry. | Six sessions and ~21 memories per episode; storage and retrieval scale are small; durable retention is low-risk. | **Does not hold.** Kivi requires observation decay, hypothesis expiry, forgetting, demotion, and suppression. Scale and harm both push toward lifecycle policies the paper never tests. | Table 1 and §3.3, p. 3; §4.1, p. 4; Appendix D, pp. 11–12. Kivi §9. |
| D11 | **What should retrieval rank as “relevant” to the current interaction?** | Semantic similarity between a BERT encoding of the current conversation context and each memory, trained with triplet loss. | Exact entity/time filters; recency; source reliability; task/permission/purpose relevance; hybrid symbolic+dense retrieval; learned end-to-end utility. | Similar language corresponds to useful memory; relevance is the dominant objective; no privacy or disclosure constraints enter ranking. | **Partly holds.** Semantic relevance is useful, but Kivi’s applicability depends on mode, memory tier, surface, entity, and permission. The paper’s ranker has no place for those constraints. | §4.2 and Eq. 1, p. 5; Appendix E.2, p. 12. Kivi §§4, 6, 7. |
| D12 | **How much of ranked memory should be exposed to the generator?** | Retrieve exactly top-1 memory, then add all associated linked memories as extended context. | Top-k; thresholded set; diversified retrieval; token-budget optimization; hierarchical retrieval; abstain below threshold; retrieve broadly and disclose selectively. | One strong seed plus neighbors covers relevant context; a fixed top-1 controls efficiency; linked neighbors are safe and useful. | **Does not hold as stated.** Kivi says retrieval and disclosure must be separable and “always retrieves everything it has,” while surface-specific gates decide use/disclosure. The paper conflates retrieval selection with what the generator sees. | §4.2, p. 5. Kivi §§1 and 6–8. |
| D13 | **When no candidate is sufficiently relevant, should the system return nothing, guess, or retrieve the nearest item anyway?** | Top-1 is always selected; no similarity threshold or retrieval abstention behavior is specified. | No-memory outcome; calibrated threshold; clarification; show near misses; uncertainty-aware retrieval. | Every synthetic context has useful seeded history often enough; a weakly related memory mainly risks awkwardness, not consequential action. | **Does not hold.** Kivi makes abstention and visible search traces first-class, because a plausible invented work answer has higher cost. The mismatch pushes toward an explicit no-result state. | §4.2, p. 5; evaluation §§5–6. Kivi §8. |
| D14 | **Should memory management and response generation share a model or be isolated components?** | One fine-tuned FLAN-T5-Large performs generation, summarization, and linking via task prefixes; a separate BERT dual encoder performs retrieval. | Separate models/services for every stage; one end-to-end model; rule-based write guards; external LLM plus structured store; independent safety classifier. | Multi-task transfer and a 1B-parameter research system outweigh modular auditability; 8×A6000 training is tolerable. | **Partly conflicts.** Kivi requires structural write-path exclusions and separate retrieval rights, which cannot rest solely on a shared generative model’s learned behavior. Paper evidence concerns dialogue quality, not boundary enforcement. | §4 and §4.1, p. 4; Appendix E, p. 12. Kivi §§2, 5, 7. |
| D15 | **What contextual inputs should the response generator receive?** | Current speakers’ identities/jobs or relationships, current conversation history, the retrieved memory, and its linked memories, serialized into one sequence. | Full raw history; only structured facts; tool state; source snippets; uncertainty and provenance; permission policy; task-specific context. | Persona/job labels and conversational text are sufficient; input fits 1,024 tokens; provenance need not be user-visible. | **Does not hold fully.** Kivi needs application context, distinct dictation/Hey Kivi pathways, provenance, tier, permission, and potentially tool state. The paper offers no interface for these. | §4.1 “Dialogue Generator,” p. 4; Appendix E.1, p. 12. Kivi §§6–8. |
| D16 | **When a memory is used, what should the user be told about its source, confidence, or withholding?** | Nothing explicit; memory is incorporated into a natural reply. Memory tags exist as dataset supervision, not an end-user explanation surface. | Inline citations; Why panel; confidence/tier labels; retrieved-but-withheld trace; source transcript; editable memory card. | Seamless, human-like conversation is the product outcome; opacity has little modeled cost. | **Does not hold—directly opposed.** Kivi makes legibility, attribution, reversibility, and withheld-memory traces part of the product contract. | §3.3 “Memory Tagging,” pp. 3–4; §5.3 evaluation criteria, p. 6. Kivi §§1, 8, 9. |
| D17 | **When the system is wrong, who detects and repairs the memory, and what persists afterward?** | **Underspecified.** The work discusses model contradiction and dataset filtering, but provides no user correction, rollback, provenance inspection, or re-derivation suppression mechanism. | Inline correction; user confirmation; source adjudication; version history; demotion; deletion plus suppression; automatic contradiction resolution. | Research episodes are short, synthetic, and regenerated; wrong output is an evaluation defect rather than a durable belief about a person. | **Does not hold.** Kivi treats correction cost and persistence as core failure mechanics. The difference pushes error handling from offline quality control into the user-facing runtime. | §6, pp. 6–8; Limitations/Ethics, p. 9; Appendix D. Kivi §§8–9. |
| D18 | **What changes over time: the memory, its confidence, its availability, or only its connections?** | New memory sentences and links accumulate across sessions; “updates” are represented by relationships among old and new sentences. No decay, confidence, consent, or policy evolution is specified. | Mutable belief state; time-bounded validity; confidence/evidence accumulation; tier promotion; decay; expiry; user-controlled suppression; policy-version reprocessing. | Six-session horizon approximates “long-term”; all sessions are comparable; observations need not age. | **Does not hold.** Kivi explicitly requires tier changes, decay, expiry, supersession, and sticky suppression. The paper’s temporal model is history accumulation, not revisable belief governance. | §§3.3–4.1, pp. 3–5; Table 1. Kivi §§4 and 9. |
| D19 | **What data should train and test the memory system when real multi-person longitudinal data are scarce?** | Generate scenarios with GPT-4, then dialogues/memories/links with ChatGPT; filter format failures; train/test on the resulting 8.5K synthetic episodes. | Human longitudinal data; consented product logs; hand-authored adversarial corpus; hybrid synthetic/real; counterfactual and privacy-focused cases. | Synthetic generators reproduce the phenomena that matter; prompt-controlled consistency is desirable; distributional realism is secondary. | **Does not hold without qualification.** Kivi’s corpus must exercise non-retention, absent answers, suppression, contradictions, sensitive drops, and distinct product surfaces—cases the paper’s generator was encouraged to smooth into coherent continuity. | §§2–3 and Table 1, pp. 2–3; Appendix C–D, pp. 11–12. Kivi Appendix C. |
| D20 | **Which generated samples should be discarded before they become training evidence?** | Drop duplicate-topic scenarios, missing required roles/events, partner-coverage failures, malformed/mismatched/very short utterances, incomplete memories, and toxic episodes. | Keep hard cases with labels; filter privacy leakage, unsupported inference, contradictions, bias, stale facts, or failed abstentions; stratify rather than discard. | Structural completeness and conversational cleanliness correlate with memory quality; malformed cases are noise, not representative failures. | **Does not hold fully.** Kivi needs transcripts that produce nothing, absent answers, contradictions, and prohibited third-party/sensitive candidates. Filtering for completeness can erase exactly the failure modes it must demonstrate. | §3, pp. 2–4; Appendix D, pp. 11–12; Ethics, p. 9. Kivi Appendix C. |
| D21 | **What should count as success for a memory system?** | Human ratings of dialogue consistency/coherence/humanness/engagingness/memorability, plus pass/fail judgments of summaries, links, and tags. | Retrieval precision/recall; calibrated abstention; contradiction resolution; privacy non-retention; provenance accuracy; correction persistence; latency/cost; user control and trust. | Natural synthetic conversation is the target; annotators can judge memory adequacy from visible episodes; harms outside dialogue quality are out of scope. | **Does not hold.** Kivi’s core claims concern boundaries, observability, correction, and non-regrowth. A fluent memorable reply can violate every one. | §§5–6, pp. 5–8; Appendix F, p. 12. Kivi Appendix C. |

## 2. Forced choices

These are load-bearing in the paper: changing them would materially alter either the claimed problem, the training signal, the architecture, or the reported outcome.

| Flag | Why it is load-bearing in the paper | Why Kivi actually has to think about it |
|---|---|---|
| **F1 — D1/D2: main-speaker, partner-indexed memory** | This is the definition of “Mixed-Session” and “Egocentric Memory,” and the claimed advantage over ordinary session summaries. The comparison in §6 and Appendix I attributes partner recognition to this partition. | Kivi is user-centered but explicitly refuses to keep other people. The useful index and the forbidden subject overlap. |
| **F2 — D3/D4: retaining emotions, thoughts, and partner details** | The ablation argues that going beyond facts to emotions and thoughts is what makes egocentric memory richer than summaries (§6, pp. 7–8). Removing those classes weakens the paper’s demonstrated contrast. | Those are specifically among Kivi’s excluded or third-party categories. This is not a parameter mismatch; it reverses the product’s trust boundary. |
| **F3 — D6/D8: natural-language memories plus binary links** | Training targets, the linker, retrieval expansion, tagging, and the dataset annotations all depend on this representation. | Kivi’s epistemic tiers and correction operations require distinctions a binary related/not-related edge cannot express. |
| **F4 — D7/D9/D18: connect updates, do not delete/update** | The authors explicitly reject direct update/deletion because of information loss and claim linked history prevents contradiction (§4.1). | Kivi needs both audit history and an authoritative current belief, plus suppression of re-derivation. Preserving history and governing present truth are separate decisions. |
| **F5 — D11/D12/D13: dense top-1 retrieval plus linked expansion** | This is the entire retrieval module and the context fed to generation. The paper attributes consistency failures of comparison models to using all memory rather than proper retrieval (Appendix H). | Kivi separates retrieval from disclosure, needs structural path restrictions, and treats no-answer behavior as first-class. Top-1 forces an answer even when nothing qualifies. |
| **F6 — D14: multi-task fine-tuning on MISC** | EMMA’s contribution is not merely a store; the single dialogue module is trained to generate, summarize, and link, and the authors argue retrieval added to general LLMs still over-focuses on memory. | Kivi’s hard exclusions and user-visible semantics may not be learnable behavior alone; isolation boundaries matter independently of fluent dialogue. |
| **F7 — D19: synthetic, coherence-seeking corpus** | MISC supplies all supervision and evaluation seeds. Its controlled continuity makes the task trainable and the reported scores possible. | Kivi’s hardest cases are discontinuities: missing answers, contradictions, sensitive content, third-party leakage, correction, and decay. Corpus construction determines whether those exist at all. |
| **F8 — D21: conversational human evaluation** | The paper’s empirical claims rest almost entirely on annotators’ perceptions; there is no end-to-end privacy, lifecycle, or calibrated-retrieval evaluation. | Kivi’s promised properties can fail invisibly while the conversation remains coherent, memorable, and engaging. |

## 3. Unexamined defaults

| Default the paper inherits | The alternative this line of work rarely tries | Why it matters here | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **More remembered personal detail is better, provided dialogue improves.** | Treat non-retention, purpose limitation, or deliberate forgetting as a positive capability and evaluate it directly. | The paper’s “richer than facts” claim treats precisely the content Kivi refuses to keep as added value. | §6 ablation, pp. 7–8; Ethics only filters toxicity, p. 9. |
| **The main speaker is entitled to store what partners disclose.** | Model consent or information ownership per claim; distinguish “needed now” from “may persist.” | Egocentric perspective answers whose memory it is, not whose information it contains. | §3.3, p. 3; Table 5, pp. 6–7. |
| **A session boundary is a legitimate consolidation boundary.** | Maintain an event-sourced candidate stream and consolidate by task, entity, confirmation, or later utility. | Kivi’s surfaces do not guarantee neat conversational sessions. | §4.1, p. 4. |
| **Natural-language summaries are adequate belief objects.** | Store atomic claims with source spans, epistemic status, temporal validity, and policy metadata; generate prose only at presentation time. | Free text hides whether something is stated, observed, hypothesized, current, or suppressed. | §§3.3–4.1, pp. 3–5. |
| **“Related” and “updated” can share one edge type.** | Make contradiction and supersession first-class relations with a rule for which version is currently usable. | Linking can preserve context without deciding truth. | §4.1, pp. 4–5; Table 15. |
| **Semantic similarity is equivalent to retrieval utility.** | Rank by expected task value subject to entity, time, authority, permission, and harm constraints. | A semantically close memory may be stale, prohibited, low-confidence, or about the wrong disclosure surface. | §4.2, p. 5. |
| **There is always a best memory worth returning.** | Allow the retriever to return an empty set and expose search coverage. | Top-1 has no representation of “the answer is absent.” | §4.2, p. 5. |
| **Memory should be visible through natural conversational use.** | Let the system know without saying; expose use/withholding separately through a trace. | The paper rewards active recall, while Kivi treats uninvited recollection as potentially intrusive. | §5.3 and Appendix F. |
| **Persona/job labels are reliable identity anchors.** | Resolve entities from attributable real-world records and represent ambiguity/merges explicitly. | Kivi will face same-name people, changing organizations, aliases, and uncertain mentions. | §3.1, pp. 2–3; §4.1, p. 4. |
| **Short synthetic episodes stand in for long-term memory.** | Evaluate over months, drift, changing roles, policy changes, deletion requests, and corpus growth. | Six sessions never create the scale or staleness pressures Kivi’s lifecycle rules address. | Table 1, p. 3; Limitations, p. 9. |
| **Offline dataset filtering is the main safety boundary.** | Enforce runtime write/read/disclosure policy and test it adversarially on every path. | Kivi’s boundary must hold on real user inputs, including content necessary for the immediate task. | Appendix D and Ethics, pp. 9, 11–12. |
| **A memory error is a dialogue-quality error.** | Treat it as a persistent state mutation requiring provenance, repair, rollback, and non-regrowth verification. | The cost of one wrong Kivi belief can extend across future work actions. | §§5–6 vs. Kivi §§8–9. |

## 4. Questions the paper never faced, raised by Kivi

These come from Kivi’s position document, not from gaps filled with generic memory-system concerns.

1. **When an input mixes the user’s words with application context written by others, which spans are eligible to cross the write boundary?** The paper’s turns belong to named synthetic speakers; it does not face transient third-party context that must be used and then forgotten. *(Kivi §§2, 5.)*
2. **When a work fact is derived from third-party content, what makes it “residue about the user’s world” rather than retained information about the third party?** *(Kivi §5.)*
3. **When a candidate touches an excluded category and a legitimate work fact at once, should the whole candidate be dropped, split, redacted, or logged without content?** *(Kivi §2.)*
4. **What evidence proves that prohibited content never reached durable storage, caches, embeddings, logs, traces, or training data—not merely that it was absent from the final memory list?** *(Kivi §§2, 5 and Appendix C claims 1–2.)*
5. **When the system has formed an observation but the current permission allows only stated facts, what may influence internal planning, what may reach the answer, and what may appear in the trace?** *(Kivi §§1, 6.)*
6. **When the user temporarily invites a hypothesis, what state marks the invitation’s scope, and how is the resulting question prevented from becoming durable memory?** *(Kivi §6.)*
7. **When the same request is ordinary dictation rather than Hey Kivi, which stores and fields are structurally unreachable rather than merely filtered?** *(Kivi §7.)*
8. **When a pronunciation/entity memory and an observation share an entity, how is graph expansion prevented from crossing the dictation boundary indirectly?** *(Derived from Kivi §§4 and 7.)*
9. **When evidence supports a recurring pattern but the user has not confirmed it, what evidence count justifies surfacing it without silently turning observation into rule?** *(Kivi §4; threshold intentionally open in Appendix B.)*
10. **When confirmation prompts are costly, what makes one uncertain memory worth spending the weekly budget on rather than another?** *(Kivi §8.)*
11. **When a user corrects a memory inline, which earlier sources remain visible, which downstream beliefs are recomputed, and which actions taken under the old belief remain auditable?** *(Kivi §§8–9.)*
12. **When the user says “That’s not me anymore,” how broad is the suppression: exact sentence, semantic pattern, evidence set, category, or time period?** *(Kivi §9.)*
13. **When future evidence resembles a suppressed pattern but is genuinely new, what distinguishes respectful non-regrowth from ignoring real change?** *(Kivi §9.)*
14. **When an observation decays, does it become retrievable-but-withheld, archival-only, or unavailable, and what does its trace show?** *(Kivi §9.)*
15. **When the system abstains, what search coverage is safe and useful to expose without leaking a withheld observation or third-party content?** *(Kivi §§6 and 8.)*
16. **When a source transcript is deleted or becomes inaccessible, what happens to memories derived solely or jointly from it?** *(Derived from Kivi’s attribution and reversibility principles, §§1 and 9.)*
17. **When a user edits a derived memory, is the edit a new stated belief, a correction of the extraction, or both—and how does reprocessing treat it?** *(Kivi §§4 and 9.)*
18. **When a memory is correct but using it would feel intrusive, what signal governs disclosure independently from truth and relevance?** *(Kivi §§1 and 6.)*
19. **When Kivi is wrong during a work action—drafting, scheduling, or recall—what failure is reversible, what requires confirmation before execution, and what trace must remain?** *(Kivi §§3, 8; tool set open in Appendix B.)*
20. **When system policy changes, should old transcripts be reprocessed under the new retention rules, and how are newly prohibited derived memories removed or proven absent?** *(Derived from Kivi’s demonstrable-boundary and revisability commitments.)*

## 5. Evaluation gap

### What the paper measured

- **MISC dialogue quality:** 300 test episodes / 1,800 sessions, rated 1–5 for consistency and coherence.
- **MISC memory annotations:** pass/fail judgments on about 6,600 memory sentences for summarization, 2,300 memory pairs for linking, and 1,000 memory–utterance tags for tagging.
- **EMMA conversations:** 200 seeded episodes generated by four EMMA instances, capped at eight turns per session, rated 1–5 for humanness, engagingness, and memorability.
- **Qualitative comparisons:** live-chat examples against MSC/ReBot, general LLM examples, and an egocentric-memory versus session-summary ablation. The paper does not report a quantitative end-to-end ablation table for alternative retrieval/update policies.

Sources: §§5–6, pp. 5–8; Appendix F, p. 12.

### Decisions its metrics cannot distinguish

| Indistinguishable alternatives under the reported evaluation | Decision IDs | Why both could score the same |
|---|---|---|
| Retain a third party’s sensitive detail vs. use it transiently and retain only work residue | D3, D4, D20 | Annotators reward accurate, contextually useful recollection; there is no non-retention or purpose-limitation metric. Retention may even increase memorability. |
| A user-stated fact vs. a model-observed pattern vs. an unconfirmed hypothesis, if all produce a plausible reply | D6, D16, D21 | The evaluation has no epistemic-tier labels, confirmation requirement, or unauthorized-inference penalty. |
| Binary links vs. typed `supports`/`contradicts`/`supersedes` relations | D7–D9 | “Linking” passes when two sentences share context or represent an update; it does not test whether the system knows which relation holds or which claim governs. |
| Keep all history forever vs. decay/expire/archive it | D10, D18 | Six sessions and ~21 memories per episode create little staleness or scale pressure; no delayed evaluation measures obsolete-memory resurfacing. |
| Top-1 retrieval with no threshold vs. calibrated empty retrieval | D12, D13 | Seeded synthetic conversations favor available relevant history; no absent-answer set or false-retrieval rate is reported. |
| Retrieve only permitted content vs. retrieve broadly and gate disclosure | D11–D13, D16 | Evaluators see the reply, not the candidate set, withheld set, or access-control trace. |
| A fluent unsupported answer vs. a sourced answer with provenance | D15–D17 | Consistency/memorability can be judged positively without checking a user-visible source trail. Memory summaries are checked against generated dialogue, not real-world truth. |
| Correction by overwriting vs. versioned supersession vs. demotion plus suppression | D7, D9, D17, D18 | No user correction event or reprocessing test exists. |
| One shared model vs. isolated components with hard write/read boundaries | D14 | Human dialogue ratings cannot verify structural isolation; equally fluent outputs are indistinguishable. |
| Partner-indexed storage vs. project/entity/event indexing, when both retrieve the right fact in a four-person episode | D1, D2, D11 | The test distribution bakes the paper’s speaker structure into scenarios and supervision, so alternative indexes are not compared on Kivi-shaped inputs. |
| Synthetic coherent cases vs. a corpus retaining hard contradictions, prohibited candidates, and missing answers | D19, D20 | Evaluation samples come from the same generated/filtered distribution; success does not establish robustness to cases removed or never generated. |
| Silent memory use vs. a visible Why panel with used/withheld/source status | D16, D17 | Explanation quality, user comprehension, editability, and trust calibration are not measured. |
| Correct recall at any latency/cost vs. bounded-latency/cost retrieval | D5, D11–D15 | The paper reports training compute but no serving latency, token/storage growth, or cost-quality tradeoff. |

### Bottom line of the evidence

The paper supplies evidence for a narrow proposition: in short, synthetic, multi-session conversations with changing partners, partner-separated natural-language memories and learned retrieval can support coherent, engaging recall. It supplies **precedent, not evidence**, for Kivi’s choices about consent, non-retention, epistemic tiers, permission-gated disclosure, abstention, provenance, correction, suppression, decay, or runtime boundary enforcement. Different answers to those questions could receive the same—or better—scores under the paper’s metrics.
