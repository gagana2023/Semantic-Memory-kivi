# Inverting CREEM: the decisions hidden inside “Ever-Evolving Memory by Blending and Refining the Past”

**Paper:** Seo Hyun Kim et al., *Ever-Evolving Memory by Blending and Refining the Past* (CREEM), arXiv:2403.04787v2, 7 April 2024.  
**Target setting:** Kivi, as specified in *The Things Kivi Comes to Know*.  
**Method:** Each item below is a genuine choice with at least two defensible answers. “Paper evidence” points to the section, algorithm, table, figure, or appendix where the answer can be checked. Where the paper does not settle an implementation detail, it is marked **underspecified**.

## Setting contrast that governs the inversion

CREEM studies an open-domain chatbot that should produce more human-like, personalized replies across five-session dialogues. Kivi is a text-based work assistant whose value is removing re-explanation while maintaining strict boundaries: retain only work-level information about the user’s world; separate retrieval from disclosure; distinguish stated facts from observations and hypotheses; expose provenance; and make every belief reversible. These are not surface differences. They change what “good memory” means, what errors cost, and what must be evaluated.

## 2. Forced choices

These are the decisions for which CREEM supplies the strongest evidence that its own answer is load-bearing. “Load-bearing” here does **not** mean Kivi should copy the answer; it means changing it would materially alter CREEM’s reported results or invalidate the causal story.

| ID | Forced choice | Why it is load-bearing in CREEM | Evidence strength and boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 / D15 | **Use only surface dialogue as the retrieval query, or add a generated high-level question?** | Removing question generation generally lowers pairwise response consistency/memorability win rates. | Direct ablation in Table 5 and §5.2. Exception: CC memorability does not follow the general trend, so the effect is not uniform. |
| F2 / D10–D12 | **Accumulate memory, delete contradictions wholesale, or classify-and-refine new/redundant/updated information?** | CREEM’s contribution and gains depend on refinement. Removing Refine lowers response results; GPTCareCall’s more aggressive deletion yields low contradiction but worse QA, supporting the retention/consistency tradeoff. | Table 3, Figure 3, Table 5, §5.1–§5.2. The evaluation does not isolate every component of the refine policy. |
| F3 / D4, D11 | **Store current-session summaries, or blend past and present into synthesized insights?** | Integration and sophistication are headline gains, and the method definition changes from \(f(D^s)\) to \(f(D^s, M_{[1,s-1]})\). | §3.2 equations (1)–(2), Table 2, §5.1. There is no clean “no blending but equal compute” ablation, so causal attribution partly relies on judge scores and system comparison. |
| F4 / D18 | **Can newly constructed current-turn insight influence the response immediately?** | Removing “Insight Infuse” generally reduces response consistency and memorability. | Table 5 and §5.2. The ablation compares access to current-turn new memory, not all possible ways of separating reasoning from durable storage. |
| F5 / D14–D16 | **Which past items enter blend/refine at all?** | Both construction and cleanup are bounded by retrieval. The authors explicitly say missed top-k items remain unhandled and that results depend on retriever quality. | §7 limitations (1)–(2). This is load-bearing by architecture and admitted limitation, though alternative retrievers are not evaluated. |
| F6 / D1 | **Update per turn or per session?** | CREEM’s same-turn insight infusion and immediate change handling require per-turn construction; switching cadence would remove access to current-turn blended insight. | Table 1; Algorithm 1; §3.3. Not directly ablated as cadence, so this is a structural inference from the pipeline. |
| F7 / D6–D8 | **Use generative free-text insights or a representation that cannot express new synthesized information?** | CREEM’s claimed “sophistication” and cross-session integration rely on generation beyond extraction. | §2.1, §3.2, Table 2. The paper does not compare against a typed atomic representation capable of derived claims, so only the need for synthesis—not free text itself—is supported. |

Kivi’s own likely forced decisions, derived from its stated product contract rather than CREEM’s results, are D2–D3 (write-path ownership and exclusions), D7–D8 (epistemic tier and provenance), D19–D20 (disclosure and surface-specific rights), and D23–D24 (user correction and reversibility). CREEM does not test these choices; they are forced by what Kivi claims to users.

## 3. Unexamined defaults

These choices appear to be inherited from the long-term dialogue literature rather than treated as decisions in the paper.

| Default the paper inherits | Where it appears | Alternative this line of work does not test here |
|---|---|---|
| **More remembered personal information is presumed to improve conversation.** | §1–§2 frames missing personal information as the problem; §4.3.1 rewards relevance, concreteness, diversity, and sophistication. | Evaluate **appropriate non-retention**: a system receives useful-but-private context, uses it transiently, and proves it did not store it. |
| **The system may autonomously turn behavior into persona.** | §2.1; §3.2; the “Sophistication” metric explicitly rewards going beyond direct information. | Keep inferred patterns in a separately typed, non-actionable state until the subject confirms them; store reasons only as questions. |
| **Retrieval relevance implies permission to disclose.** | §3.3 and Appendix A.4 tell the responder to reflect memory, with no disclosure stage. | Retrieve broadly for reasoning/audit, then independently gate what may be said according to user permission and memory tier. |
| **Every participant is a legitimate memory subject.** | MSC/CC are two-speaker datasets; prompts create speaker-specific insights without consent boundaries. | Treat non-user people as context-only unless an explicit work-level relation is retained without their personal content. |
| **Contradiction is primarily a storage-cleanliness problem.** | §1, §2.2, §3.2, contradiction metric. | Model contradiction as temporal change, source disagreement, ambiguity, or unresolved conflict; preserve history rather than optimize the active store for pairwise non-contradiction. |
| **Deletion is an acceptable primitive without a user-visible event history.** | §3.2 and Algorithm 1. | Append correction/supersession events and derive the active view, making every destructive-seeming operation reversible. |
| **Generated prose is an adequate memory database.** | §3.2, Appendix A.2–A.3. | Use atomic claims with types, evidence edges, temporal validity, and suppression records; generate prose only at presentation time. |
| **The model that writes memory can also judge its relationship to prior memory.** | One Blend & Refine prompt generates the insight, labels it, and identifies removals. | Separate candidate generation, entailment/conflict detection, policy enforcement, and mutation authorization so correlated model errors do not directly delete state. |
| **The latest turn is the correct center of retrieval intent.** | Appendix A.1 says focus mainly on the last utterance; Algorithm 1 uses the last two utterances plus session context. | Let explicit task structure, referenced artifacts, current application, or user-selected scope determine retrieval intent. |
| **Generic dense semantic similarity is enough for memory retrieval.** | Contriever in §4.2 and Appendix E.1. | First enforce subject, source, tier, status, temporal validity, and product-surface constraints; rank eligible items with hybrid evidence-aware retrieval. |
| **A dialogue-quality evaluator can stand in for the affected user.** | G-Eval and pairwise ChatGPT evaluation in §4.3.1 and §4.3.4. | Measure whether users understand, contest, correct, and calibrate trust in the system’s memory—and whether corrections persist. |
| **A five-session persona benchmark is a meaningful proxy for long-term memory.** | §4.1; §7 admits the dataset shortage. | Evaluate months of accumulation, sparse recurrence, entity churn, contradictory external sources, and repeated re-derivation after correction. |
| **Fluent use of memory is success even when its source is invisible.** | Appendix A.4; Table 4–5. | Require attributable response claims and score unsupported personalization or creepy disclosure as failures even when fluent and consistent. |

## 4. Questions the paper never faced, derived from Kivi

These arise from Kivi’s users, contracts, and failure modes—not from gaps in CREEM alone.

### Retention and subject boundaries

1. **When one request contains the user’s dictation, another person’s message, and application metadata, which source and subject combinations may cross the durable write boundary?**
2. **When a work-level fact necessarily names another person, what distinguishes permissible relational residue (“Priya is the Acme contact”) from impermissible retention about that person?**
3. **When a candidate falls into both a permitted work category and an excluded sensitive category, which classification controls, and how is the drop made visible without restating the sensitive content?**
4. **When excluded content influences a generated work artifact transiently, what evidence should demonstrate that it did not influence durable memory?**

### Epistemic status and evidence

5. **When the user states a preference indirectly while composing an artifact, what makes it “stated” rather than merely “observed”?**
6. **When repeated behavior supports an observation but the supporting examples differ by audience or channel, should the pattern be global, scoped, split, or withheld?**
7. **When a source transcript is edited or deleted, what happens to memories whose evidential basis depends on it?**
8. **When one memory is derived from several sources with different reliability, how should the interface communicate the basis without turning the user into a database administrator?**
9. **When a user confirms a synthesized statement, does confirmation validate its wording, all its component claims, its scope, or only permission to act on it?**

### Disclosure and product surfaces

10. **When a retrieved memory is eligible to inform an answer but not to be disclosed in the current mode, what forms of silent influence are permitted?** For example, formatting a client email versus mentioning the observed pattern that motivated the formatting.
11. **When ordinary dictation and Hey Kivi occur in the same interaction, which boundary determines whether only lexical/entity memory or broader semantic memory can be loaded?**
12. **When Anbu withholds an observation but the result would materially differ if the observation were used, what should the trace reveal without leaking the observation itself?**
13. **When a per-request Daari invitation produces a hypothesis, which parts may appear in logs, traces, telemetry, or temporary caches even though the answer is not written to memory?**
14. **When the user changes the permission dial, does the change apply prospectively, to the current request, to stored memories, or to historical traces?**

### Correction, suppression, and change

15. **When the user says “that’s not me anymore,” what exactly is suppressed: a proposition, a generalized pattern, a source set, a scope, or semantically similar future derivations?**
16. **When new post-correction behavior again supports a suppressed observation, what evidence or user action is required before it may reappear?**
17. **When “forget” conflicts with the need to preserve an auditable operation history, what is retained about the deletion without retaining the deleted belief?**
18. **When a correction arrives inline during a task, should the current output be regenerated, the memory updated, the old version superseded, or all three—and in what order?**
19. **When stated memories contradict each other, what determines whether the later statement supersedes the earlier one or whether Kivi asks because the difference may be contextual?**

### Abstention, harm, and operating constraints

20. **When Kivi lacks the requested fact but has nearby memories, what threshold distinguishes a useful evidence-backed alternative from an insinuating or privacy-leaking one?**
21. **When the cost of a false memory varies by action—misspelling a name, sending a client email, changing a deadline—how should action risk affect retrieval, confirmation, and abstention?**
22. **When confirmation prompts are budgeted, what determines “highest value” without privileging intrusive or psychologically loaded candidates?**
23. **When a memory operation fails partway through—response generated, new insight stored, old memory not superseded—what consistency guarantee should the user observe?**
24. **When multiple devices or concurrent requests update the same memory, what wins and how are conflicting corrections surfaced?**
25. **When the store becomes large, what evidence is required that excluded, forgotten, superseded, or third-party-derived material cannot re-enter through embeddings, summaries, caches, or reconstruction?**

| Evaluation | What it distinguishes | What it rewards or penalizes | Key limitation for using it as Kivi evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **G-Eval memory scores** (§4.3.1, Table 2) | Differences in integration, consistency, sophistication, relevance, concreteness, and diversity. | Coherent, detailed, varied, speaker-related, cross-session synthesized memory. | It can reward exactly what Kivi may forbid: unsupported high-level inference, greater persona capture, and retention of sensitive or third-party content. It does not score provenance, consent, reversibility, epistemic tier, or appropriate forgetting. |
| **Pairwise NLI contradiction probability** (§4.3.2, Table 3) | Whether active free-text memory pairs look contradictory to an MNLI-tuned RoBERTa model. | Fewer surface-semantic contradictions. | Deleting both sides, deleting history, or avoiding useful detail can score well. It cannot tell correct supersession from destructive information loss, contextual differences, or unresolved source conflict. |
| **Changed-information multiple-choice QA** (§4.3.3, Figure 3; Appendix B.2–B.3) | Whether the memory supports selecting the latest changed state rather than an older state. | Retaining updated information sufficient for a model to answer. | Questions are generated from full dialogues by ChatGPT and answered by ChatGPT; the prompt encourages inference. It does not distinguish direct evidence from model inference, or reversible history from latest-only replacement. |
| **BLEU, ROUGE-L, BERTScore** (§4.3.4, Table 4) | Lexical/semantic similarity to one reference response. | Reference-like wording/content. | The paper itself notes that diverse valid replies weaken these metrics. They say nothing about disclosure permission, creepiness, provenance, abstention, or correction. |
| **ChatGPT pairwise response evaluation** (§4.3.4, Table 5; Appendix B.4) | Which response is more consistent with prior sessions and recalls more past events. | Visible use of memory and lack of contradiction. | “Memorability” structurally favors saying remembered things; it cannot detect the Kivi distinction between knowing and saying. No human/user evaluation or blinding details sufficient to assess personalization harms are reported. |
| **Operation ratios** (Appendix D, Table 6) | Frequency of New, Redundant, Updated labels. | Nothing directly; used to show refinement executes. | Operation occurrence is not operation correctness. It cannot show that the right old memory was removed or that the merged insight was faithful. |
| **Ablations** (§5.2, Table 5) | Effects of removing generated-question retrieval, Refine, or same-turn insight infusion. | End-response consistency/memorability under the same judge. | Useful for CREEM’s internal components, but still blind to Kivi’s safety, governance, lifecycle, and user-control decisions. |

### Decisions for which CREEM’s metrics cannot distinguish the answers

If two systems choose different answers below, they can plausibly receive the same CREEM score. In these cases the paper supplies a precedent, not evidence for Kivi’s choice.

| Decision(s) | Two materially different answers that CREEM’s evaluation may score the same | Why the metrics are blind |
|---|---|---|
| D2–D3 | Retain a coworker’s personal circumstance versus discard it after using it transiently. | Both can produce the same next reply; no retention-boundary or privacy audit exists. Greater “concreteness” may even favor retention. |
| D4, D7 | Store “the user avoids conflict” as fact versus store it as an unconfirmed hypothesis/question. | G-Eval sees sophistication/relevance, not epistemic grammar or action rights. |
| D6, D8 | Store one source-free prose insight versus an atomic claim linked to every transcript. | Response and memory-text judges do not inspect derivation metadata or source fidelity. |
| D9–D10 | Delete an old state versus supersede it while preserving history. | Both expose only the latest active fact and answer changed-state QA identically. |
| D11 | Lossily merge repeated memories versus consolidate one claim while retaining all evidence links. | If final prose is similar, all reported metrics are equal; evidence preservation is invisible. |
| D12–D13 | Forget by hard delete versus forget plus suppression against re-derivation. | The benchmark does not replay the same source after user rejection and has no user deletion action. |
| D14 | Repair contradictions globally versus only inside retrieved top-k, when tested contradictions happen to be retrieved. | Short episodes and observed queries may not expose the unretrieved stale tail. |
| D16–D17 | Rank by semantic similarity alone versus enforce subject, permission, status, and time filters before ranking. | If benchmark data lacks adversarial cross-person or disallowed memories, both retrieve the same items. |
| D18 | Use a provisional insight for same-turn reasoning but store only after validation versus immediately store and use the same generated insight. | Current response is identical; no later audit of durable state or validation transition is scored. |
| D19 | Mention every relevant memory versus use it silently or withhold it according to a permission dial. | Memorability favors mention; there is no appropriateness-of-disclosure criterion. |
| D20 | Give dictation and interactive requests separate retrieval rights versus one universal pipeline. | The datasets contain only conversational chatbot turns, not dictation contracts. |
| D21 | Provide a provenance/withholding trace versus expose only a fluent response. | No interface, comprehension, or trust-calibration study exists. |
| D22 | Guess plausibly versus abstain with searched-nearby evidence. | Reference similarity may reward the guess; QA says “try hard to infer”; there is no false-assertion cost or calibrated-abstention metric. |
| D23–D24 | Autonomous deletion with no rollback versus user-governed versioned correction. | There are no correction tasks, recovery tests, or audit-log checks. |
| D25 | Keep an old observation forever versus decay it after non-recurrence. | Five sessions are too short and change is tested through explicit dialogue updates, not silent drift. |
| D26 | Four expensive stages per turn versus selective/asynchronous calls with equivalent outputs. | No latency, token, energy, or monetary cost is reported. |
| D27 | A method correct at 25 utterances versus one correct after years of accumulation. | Evaluation scale is about five sessions and roughly 25 utterances per episode; the authors explicitly defer longer datasets. |

### What the paper’s evidence does transfer

Without recommending a Kivi answer, the results provide evidence for three narrower propositions:

1. **A retrieval query derived from latent conversational intent can surface useful memories missed by surface similarity alone** (§5.2, Table 5), although the benefit varies by dataset/criterion.
2. **Optimizing only for low contradiction can destroy useful information**: GPTCareCall sometimes has lower contradiction than CREEM while performing worse on changed-information QA (§5.1, Table 3, Figure 3).
3. **Making current-turn, cross-session synthesis available before response generation can change response quality** (§5.2, Table 5), though the paper does not establish that the synthesized information should automatically become durable memory.

Everything else central to Kivi—non-retention of others, sensitive-category exclusions, epistemic tiers, permissioned disclosure, provenance, correction, suppression, auditability, abstention, and route-specific memory rights—remains unevaluated.
